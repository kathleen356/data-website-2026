5.4 Interpreting Line Graphs

A line graph shows how one thing changes over time. Each dot is a reading, and the line joining the dots lets us spot when values go up, down, or stay the same — all in one glance. Read the dots, follow the line, and answer the questions.

Big idea 1

What is a line graph?

What is a line graph?

A line graph uses points joined by lines to show how a value changes over time. We plot each reading as a point, then join the points in order from left to right.

Line graphs are best for data that changes over time — like temperature through a day, or a plant growing each week.

When should I use one?

Use a line graph when the horizontal axis is something continuous that flows in order — hours, days, weeks, months. If the categories are just separate groups (favourite colour, eye colour), a column graph fits better.

Ask: “Does it make sense to join these points with a line?” If yes → line graph.

Big idea 2

The features of a line graph

Every line graph should have these parts. A graph is only finished when someone else can read it without asking you a single question.

🏷️ Title

Tells us what the graph is about.

e.g. “Temperature on Monday”.

↔️ Horizontal axis (x-axis)

Usually shows the time — the thing that changes along the bottom.

e.g. Time, Day, Week, Month.

↕️ Vertical axis (y-axis)

Shows what is being measured, with an even scale.

e.g. Temperature (°C), Snowfall (cm).

⚫ Plot points

A dot marks each reading, where the two values meet.

Not “dots” or “circles” — plot points.

📈 The line

Joins the plot points in order to show the trend.

🔎 Even scale

Numbers go up the y-axis in equal steps (0, 2, 4, 6…).

TeacherCommon exam question: “What are the dots on this graph called?” → plot points. Drill the vocabulary: title, axes, scale, plot points, line.

Big idea 3

Reading a value off the line

To read a point: start on the horizontal axis, go straight up to the line, then straight across to the vertical axis to read the number.

  1. Find the time you want on the horizontal axis.
  2. Move your finger straight up until you reach the plotted point.
  3. Move straight across to the vertical axis and read the value.
  4. To find a difference or total, read each value first, then add or subtract.
TeacherModel the “up then across” finger trace on the board once. It stops the most common error — reading the wrong axis.

1a · What were the highest and lowest temperatures recorded?

  1. What was the highest temperature recorded, and at what time?
  2. What was the lowest temperature recorded, and at what time?

1b · How much snow fell between Friday and Saturday?

  1. How much snow fell between Friday and Saturday?

2a · Which months saw more than 4 mm of rain?

  1. Which months saw more than 4 mm of rain?

2b · In which weeks were the temperatures 30 °C or above?

  1. In which weeks were the temperatures 30 °C or above?
✓ Answers
  • 1a Highest / lowest: highest = 24 °C at 15:00 (3 pm); lowest = 10 °C at 6:00 (6 am).
  • 1b Fri → Sat: Friday 16 cm, Saturday 16 cm → 16 − 16 = 0 cm (no new snow fell — the line is flat).
  • 2a Rain > 4 mm: January (10), February (13), March (8), September (5), November (5) and December (8). Values of 4 or below do not count.
  • 2b 30 °C or above: Week 2 (30), Week 5 (30) and Week 6 (32).
Teacher1b is the classic trap — a flat line means no change, not “16 cm”. Push students to read both points and subtract. For 2a, “more than 4” vs “4 or more” is a language point — link it to > and ≥.
🟠 Amber Identifying the features of a line graph Name & complete features

Growth of a newborn cavoodle puppy

  1. Fill in the feature that is missing from this line graph.
  2. What is the title of this graph?
  3. What are the dots on this graph called?
  4. Which of the following data sets would be suitable for a line graph? Tick all that apply.
✓ Answers & guidance
  • 1 Missing feature: the line joining the plot points is missing — the points have been plotted but not connected. (A finished line graph joins the points in order from left to right.)
  • 2 Title: “Growth of newborn cavoodle puppy”.
  • 3 The dots: they are called plot points (not “dot points” or “circles”).
  • 4 Line-graph data: “The daily temperature in Darwin in July”, “How many pies are ordered from the canteen each month”, and “Growth of a sunflower plant in three weeks” — these change over time. Favourite colour and eye colour are categories, so a column graph suits them better.
TeacherThe missing feature here is the line — the points are plotted but not joined. This is the “add missing features to complete a line graph” success criterion. Drill the vocabulary: plot points, not dots/circles.

The ice cream parlour

The table shows the number of customers each lunchtime at an ice cream parlour.

Lunchtime customers
DayCustomers
Monday42
Tuesday47
Wednesday3
Thursday36

(a) Draw the line graph using the tool in the “Draw one” section above, then answer these:

  1. (b) On which day did the ice cream parlour have the most customers?
  2. (c) Work out how many customers visited the ice cream parlour in total.
  3. (d) Give a possible reason why there were only 3 visitors on Wednesday.
✓ Answers & guidance
  • (a) Graph: plot points at Mon 42, Tue 47, Wed 3, Thu 36, Fri 40, Sat 31 and join them in order. A scale of 1 square = 5 keeps it readable (largest value 47). Don’t forget a title and both axis labels.
  • (b) Most customers: Tuesday (47) — the highest point on the graph.
  • (c) Total: 42 + 47 + 3 + 36 + 40 + 31 = 199 customers.
  • (d) Possible reason (any sensible one): the parlour may have been closed part of Wednesday, the weather was cold/rainy, a public holiday, roadworks, or a staff shortage. Accept any reason that would reduce customers.
TeacherPart (c) is 2 marks — students must total all six days (199), not just read the graph. Part (d) accepts any reasonable real-world explanation; look for a link between the reason and fewer customers.
